PLANT LIBRARY / BOTANICAL PROFILE

Ginger

Zingiber officinale

Aromatic, warming, and at home in kitchens around the world, ginger is a rhizome with a long culinary and traditional history.

Evidence note: This profile is educational. It does not establish that this plant treats, prevents, or cures disease. Evidence may be limited and specific to particular preparations.

Botanical overview

Ginger is the underground stem (rhizome) of a tropical flowering plant in the Zingiberaceae family. It is used fresh, dried, powdered, pickled, and cooked.

Nutritional properties & naturally occurring compounds

Ginger contains pungent compounds including gingerols and shogaols. Culinary servings are generally small, so it is not usually a major source of calories or macronutrients.

Traditional uses

Ginger has been used in food and traditional practices across Asia and elsewhere, including preparations associated with digestive comfort and nausea. Historical use does not by itself demonstrate clinical effectiveness.

What does research say?

Some clinical research has examined ginger for nausea and other specific outcomes. Findings depend on population, dose, and preparation; results for one use should not be generalized to every condition or product.

Common preparations

Fresh ginger can be sliced into hot water, grated into meals, or added to soups and marinades. Dried ginger is used in baking and spice blends. Concentrated extracts differ from ordinary culinary use.

Safety considerations

Larger supplemental amounts may cause heartburn or stomach upset. People taking anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines, those with gallstone conditions, and people who are pregnant or preparing for surgery should ask a clinician before using concentrated supplements.
