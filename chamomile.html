PLANT LIBRARY / BOTANICAL PROFILE

Chamomile

Matricaria chamomilla

A familiar daisy-like flower often enjoyed as a fragrant herbal infusion, chamomile has a long place in traditional home practices.

Evidence note: This profile is educational. It does not establish that this plant treats, prevents, or cures disease. Evidence may be limited and specific to particular preparations.

Botanical overview

German chamomile is an annual flowering plant in the Asteraceae family. Roman chamomile is a different species; products labeled simply chamomile may not distinguish them.

Nutritional properties & naturally occurring compounds

Chamomile is usually consumed as an infusion rather than as a meaningful source of calories or nutrients. Its flowers contain volatile oils and flavonoids, including apigenin-related compounds.

Traditional uses

Chamomile has been used traditionally for digestive complaints, relaxation, and topical soothing preparations. A history of use does not prove effectiveness for a particular condition.

What does research say?

Research has explored chamomile for several outcomes, but studies are often small or use different preparations. Evidence is not strong enough to treat it as a replacement for established care.

Common preparations

Dried flowers are commonly steeped in hot water for tea. Chamomile also appears in topical products and extracts; these forms are not interchangeable.

Safety considerations

Chamomile can cause allergic reactions, especially in people sensitive to ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds, or related plants. Possible interactions have been reported with some medicines. Ask a clinician before medicinal use during pregnancy or while taking medicines.
